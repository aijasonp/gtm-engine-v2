# SCQA Writing Framework

## Overview

SCQA is a consulting-grade communication structure developed at McKinsey and BCG. It makes complex ideas easy to follow by grounding the reader before introducing tension — and resolving it with a clear answer.

**S** = Situation → Set the context. What does the reader already know or believe?  
**C** = Complication → Introduce the problem, tension, or change that disrupts the situation.  
**Q** = Question → The natural question the complication raises. What does the reader now want to know?  
**A** = Answer → Your solution, insight, or recommendation. The direct resolution.

Applied to B2B outbound: SCQA stops cold emails from opening with "I hope this finds you well." It opens with *their world*, surfaces *their tension*, and positions your solution as the inevitable answer.

---

## Instructions

When activated, follow this process for any writing request:

### Step 1 — Gather context
Ask for (or infer from available data):
- **Who is the reader?** (title, company, industry, known pain)
- **What outcome do you want?** (reply, meeting, click, belief change)
- **What do you know about their current situation?** (recent news, trigger, tech stack, role)
- **Which value prop from the product profile is most relevant to them?**

### Step 2 — Build the SCQA skeleton
Draft each element explicitly before writing the final piece:

| Element | Prompt to self |
|---------|---------------|
| **Situation** | What is universally true about their world right now? (No tension yet — this is common ground.) |
| **Complication** | What has changed, broken, or become urgent? Why is the status quo now costly? |
| **Question** | What question does this naturally raise for them? (Often unstated — the reader feels it.) |
| **Answer** | What do we offer that directly resolves this question? Be specific, not generic. |

### Step 3 — Write the output
Translate the skeleton into the requested format:
- **Cold email:** S in line 1-2, C in line 3-4, Q implied, A = CTA + value prop (under 120 words total)
- **LinkedIn post:** S in hook, C as body tension, Q as turning point, A as insight + CTA
- **Executive summary:** S as background section, C as problem statement, Q as "what must we address?", A as recommendation
- **Pitch narrative:** S as market context, C as gap/failure mode, Q as "why now?", A as our approach

### Step 4 — Critique and tighten
Before delivering:
- Is the Situation genuinely common ground, or does it feel like a lecture?
- Is the Complication specific to *them*, or generic to everyone?
- Does the Answer directly resolve the Question — or does it drift into product features?
- Is the total length appropriate for the format?

---

## Constraints

- Never open cold emails with "I hope this finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," or any filler opener.
- The Complication must be specific — name the actual pain, not a vague category.
- The Answer is not a product dump. One clear value statement + one proof point maximum for cold outreach.
- Situation should be 1-2 sentences max in outreach contexts. The reader doesn't have time for setup.
- Always write from the reader's perspective — their world, their problem, their gain.
- Tie the Answer to one differentiator or proof point from the product profile. Never invent one.

## Example (cold email, VP of Operations at a large logistics company)

**Situation:** Most operations teams are being asked to ship more changes with the same headcount.
**Complication:** But every change still runs through tickets and manual steps, so the backlog grows faster than the team.
**Question:** How do you speed up changes without adding risk or people?
**Answer:** [Product] turns the repeat changes into approved, self-service workflows, so the team handles exceptions instead of every request.

> **Rendered email:**
> "Your team is probably being asked to move faster on changes without adding headcount. Most of that time goes to repeat requests that still run through tickets and manual steps.
>
> [Product] turns those repeat changes into approved, self-service workflows. Your team keeps control and stops being the queue.
>
> Open to hearing how other ops teams set this up?"
